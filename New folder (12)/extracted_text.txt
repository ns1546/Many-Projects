Abstract
Federated Learning (FL) enables collaborative medical image diagnosis without sharing
raw patient data, supporting privacy regulations such as HIPAA and GDPR. In practice,
medical FL faces non-IID data drift, communication bottlenecks, and gradient-leakage
risks. Existing studies often address these issues separately and rarely evaluate full
pipelines under edge constraints.
This report presents FEDMED-X, a communication-efficient and privacy-preserving
federated learning system for medical image classification. The framework integrates
FedProx (µ = 0.01), DP-SGD (ϵ = 1.0, δ = 1e−5), TopK 50% + INT8 compression,
and a lightweight MedNet-CBAM model designed for edge deployment.
On MedMNIST PneumoniaMNIST with 5 Dirichlet non-IID clients (α = 0.5), Fed-
Prox reached 85.58% accuracy. With privacy and compression together, FEDMED-X
achieved 75.16% accuracy while reducing communication by 90% under ϵ = 1.0. Edge-
oriented evaluation targets NVIDIA Jetson Nano deployment with 0.20 ms/image in-
ference latency.
Keywords: Federated Learning; Differential Privacy; Edge Computing; Medical Image
Classification; FedProx; TopK; Jetson Nano.

Chapter 1
1
Introduction
Artificial Intelligence (AI) has transformed medical image analysis by enabling auto-
mated detection of diseases from radiological scans such as chest X-rays. Deep learning
models can support clinicians in identifying pneumonia and related abnormalities with
high speed and consistency. However, building robust medical AI systems requires large
and diverse datasets collected across multiple hospitals. In practice, hospitals cannot
freely pool patient images into a single central repository because of legal, ethical, and
security constraints.
Privacy regulations such as the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability
Act (HIPAA) and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) restrict the trans-
fer of identifiable medical data. As a result, conventional centralized machine learn-
ing pipelines face a fundamental conflict:
high model performance requires multi-
institutional data, while privacy law requires data localization.
Federated Learning
(FL) was introduced to resolve this conflict by allowing institutions to train a shared
model collaboratively while keeping raw patient data inside each hospital [1], [2].
Despite strong theoretical promise, real-world medical FL deployments remain dif-
ficult.
Recent multi-center studies report that hospital firewalls, bandwidth limits,
non-Independent and Identically Distributed (non-IID) data drift, and residual gradi-
ent leakage frequently block practical translation [3], [4], [5]. In resource-constrained
settings such as rural Bangladesh, these barriers are more severe because clinics often
lack high-end computing infrastructure and stable high-bandwidth networks. Therefore,
medical FL systems must be simultaneously accurate, private, communication-efficient,
and edge-deployable.
1


--- PAGE 21 ---

This capstone project presents FEDMED-X, a unified federated learning pipeline
for medical image classification that jointly integrates FedProx optimization, Differen-
tial Privacy via DP-SGD, TopK+INT8 compression, and a lightweight MedNet-CBAM
neural backbone for edge-oriented deployment. Rather than proposing an isolated al-
gorithm, FEDMED-X contributes a systems-level evaluation of the privacy–utility–
communication trade-off under realistic non-IID hospital partitions.
1.1
Problem Statement
The central problem addressed in this research is the absence of a practical, end-to-end
federated medical imaging pipeline that can operate under joint real-world constraints.
Existing solutions typically optimize only one dimension of the problem. Some methods
improve non-IID robustness, some add privacy noise, some compress communication
payloads, and some evaluate edge inference. Very few systems evaluate all of these
requirements together under a common experimental protocol.
In clinical environments, this gap creates four concrete failures:
1. Privacy failure: Centralized training requires raw image aggregation and increases
exposure to data breaches.
2. Heterogeneity failure: Hospital datasets differ by patient population, scanner
type, and disease prevalence, causing standard FedAvg models to drift.
3. Communication failure: Full-precision gradient exchange is expensive and often
blocked by institutional firewalls.
4. Deployment failure: Heavy models and privacy mechanisms are difficult to run
on low-cost edge devices suitable for rural clinics.
2


--- PAGE 22 ---

Therefore, the research problem is not only to classify medical images, but to design
a federated system that remains usable when privacy, non-IID drift, bandwidth limits,
and edge hardware constraints are enforced at the same time.
1.2
Problem Background
Medical imaging AI has achieved strong benchmark performance under centralized
training.
However, healthcare data are naturally fragmented.
Each hospital stores
its own patient records, and cross-hospital transfer is restricted by regulation and in-
stitutional policy [1]. FL addresses this by exchanging model updates instead of raw
images [2].
Unfortunately, recent clinical evidence shows that FL prototypes often fail during
translation. Bujotzek et al. demonstrated that infrastructure, governance, and data-
management bottlenecks remain major obstacles in multi-hospital radiology FL [3].
Schoenpflug et al. reported that network restrictions and long communication delays
significantly affect multi-country pathology FL deployments [4]. Koutsoubis et al. fur-
ther emphasized that keeping data local is not equivalent to full privacy, because shared
gradients can still leak sensitive information [5].
From an algorithmic perspective, non-IID data remains a dominant challenge. Riyadi
et al. showed that FedProx is more robust than FedAvg under Dirichlet label skew on
MedMNIST tasks [6].
At the same time, surveys highlight weak clinical and edge
deployment benchmarks in current medical FL literature [7], [8]. These findings col-
lectively motivate a system that combines non-IID-aware optimization, formal privacy,
communication compression, and edge-oriented model design.
1.3
Research Objectives
The objectives of this study are as follows:
3


--- PAGE 23 ---

(i) To design a federated learning framework that enables multiple simulated hospi-
tals to collaboratively train a shared medical image classifier without exchanging
raw patient data.
(ii) To mitigate non-IID client drift using FedProx regularization with proximal co-
efficient µ = 0.01.
(iii) To enforce formal privacy protection through DP-SGD with target privacy budget
ϵ = 1.0 and δ = 1 × 10−5.
(iv) To reduce communication overhead by applying TopK 50% gradient sparsification
and INT8 quantization, targeting approximately 90% bandwidth reduction.
(v) To evaluate the complete pipeline through a factorial ablation study and assess
edge-oriented deployability using a lightweight MedNet-CBAM model suitable for
NVIDIA Jetson Nano class hardware.
1.4
Motivations
This research is motivated by three complementary needs.
First, there is a clinical motivation. Bangladesh and many low-resource regions
face shortages of specialist radiologists. Affordable AI tools deployed at the edge can
support faster triage of chest X-rays in rural clinics, provided patient privacy is pre-
served.
Second, there is a technical motivation.
Most published medical FL studies
evaluate components in isolation. A practical system must quantify what happens when
privacy noise, compression, and non-IID optimization are applied together. Without
this joint evaluation, reported accuracies can be misleading for real deployment.
Third, there is an academic and societal motivation. Trustworthy AI, privacy-
preserving learning, and edge intelligence are priority themes in modern computing
4


--- PAGE 24 ---

research and graduate study pathways.
FEDMED-X is designed as a reproducible
systems benchmark that connects algorithm design with deployment reality.
1.5
Significance of the Research
The significance of FEDMED-X can be summarized in four dimensions:
• Scientific significance: It provides a controlled multi-objective evaluation of ac-
curacy, privacy budget, communication cost, and edge suitability under non-IID
medical FL settings.
• Engineering significance: It converts known components (FedProx, DP-SGD,
TopK, CBAM) into a coherent pipeline with explicit trade-off measurement.
• Healthcare significance: It supports privacy-preserving collaborative learning
that can benefit hospitals unwilling or unable to share raw data.
• Educational significance: It demonstrates complex engineering problem-solving
across machine learning, distributed systems, privacy mathematics, and embedded
deployment constraints.
1.6
Research Contributions
The major contributions of this capstone research are:
1. Unified systems pipeline: Development of FEDMED-X, integrating FedProx,
DP-SGD, TopK+INT8 compression, and MedNet-CBAM in one reproducible feder-
ated medical classification framework.
2. Factorial ablation protocol: Design and execution of a five-run ablation matrix
(FedAvg, FedProx, FedProx+DP, FedProx+TopK/INT8, and full FEDMED-X) to
isolate privacy and compression effects.
5


--- PAGE 25 ---

3. Empirical trade-off evidence: Demonstration that FedProx improves non-IID
robustness over FedAvg (85.58% vs 84.46% accuracy), while full privacy and com-
pression constraints yield 75.16% accuracy with 90% bandwidth reduction under
ϵ = 1.0.
4. Edge-oriented design: Construction of a lightweight, Opacus-compatible GroupNorm-
based MedNet-CBAM backbone targeting resource-constrained deployment scenar-
ios such as NVIDIA Jetson Nano.
5. Deployment-aware framing: Positioning the work as a systems evaluation con-
tribution rather than an unsupported absolute “first invention” claim, aligned with
current literature gaps in edge and clinical translation [7], [8].
1.7
Complex Engineering Analysis

Background Study
This chapter establishes the theoretical and empirical foundation of FEDMED-X. First,
it reviews the major research streams relevant to federated medical image classifica-
tion: non-IID robust optimization, privacy-preserving learning, communication-efficient
model updates, and edge-oriented deployment. Second, it analyzes the practical prob-
lem space and identifies the systems gap that motivates the proposed framework. The
goal of this chapter is not only to summarize prior work, but to show why isolated so-
lutions are insufficient for real hospital constraints and why a joint evaluation pipeline
is required.
2.1
Literature Review
Federated Learning (FL) has become a central paradigm for collaborative healthcare AI
because it enables multi-institutional training without direct exchange of raw patient
images [1]. In medical imaging, this property is especially important due to strict legal
and ethical constraints on data movement. Over the last several years, the literature
has expanded from basic aggregation methods toward specialized solutions for statisti-
cal heterogeneity, formal privacy, communication bottlenecks, and deployment realism.
The following subsections synthesize these directions and position FEDMED-X within
the resulting research landscape.
2.1.1
Federated Learning for Medical Imaging
Guan and Liu provide a comprehensive survey of federated medical image analysis and
organize the field around system architecture, privacy mechanisms, and communication
9


--- PAGE 29 ---

challenges [1].
Their review makes clear that medical FL is not a single-algorithm
problem; it is a multi-constraint systems problem. Ghosh et al. further emphasize that
despite rapid publication growth, clinical translation remains limited and deployment-
oriented benchmarks are still weak [7]. Zhou et al. reinforce this point by arguing that
fair evaluation of medical FL should include not only accuracy, but also communication
and computational efficiency [8].
These surveys collectively indicate a shift in research expectations. A method that
reports only centralized-style accuracy under simplified conditions is no longer sufficient.
Modern medical FL research is increasingly judged by whether it remains robust under
non-IID partitions, whether it supports privacy constraints, and whether it can operate
under practical resource limits.
2.1.2
Non-IID Heterogeneity and FedProx
A major obstacle in multi-hospital learning is non-IID data.
Different institutions
observe different class distributions, acquisition protocols, and patient demographics.
Under such heterogeneity, standard Federated Averaging (FedAvg) often suffers from
client drift and unstable convergence. Li et al. introduced FedProx to address heteroge-
neous federated optimization by adding a proximal term that penalizes local deviation
from the global model [2]. This modification is particularly relevant to clinical networks
where local datasets may be small, unbalanced, or distributionally skewed.
Riyadi et al. provide direct empirical support for FedProx in medical imaging bench-
marks. In a comparative study on MedMNIST tasks under Dirichlet label skew, Fed-
Prox demonstrated stronger robustness and stability than FedAvg and other selected
baselines [6].
This evidence motivates the use of FedProx as the core optimizer in
FEDMED-X. However, Riyadi et al. do not jointly evaluate formal differential privacy,
10


--- PAGE 30 ---

aggressive update compression, and edge deployment constraints. Therefore, their re-
sults establish FedProx as a strong non-IID baseline, but not as a complete deployment
solution.
2.1.3
Privacy Risks and Differential Privacy
A common misconception in federated healthcare systems is that data locality automat-
ically guarantees privacy. In reality, shared gradients and model updates can still leak
sensitive information through reconstruction and membership-inference style attacks.
Koutsoubis et al. highlight this residual risk and argue for stronger privacy-preserving
FL designs in medical imaging [5].
Abadi et al.
established Differentially Private
Stochastic Gradient Descent (DP-SGD) as a foundational method for training deep
networks with formal privacy guarantees by clipping gradients and injecting calibrated
noise abadi2016deep.
In the medical FL literature, privacy-preserving methods vary widely in strength and
practicality. Some frameworks rely on cryptographic techniques such as homomorphic
encryption, while others employ formal DP budgets. Yesmin proposed MedHE, com-
bining communication efficiency with privacy-preserving mechanisms including DP and
homomorphic encryption [9]. Pachlasiya and Patel reported private multi-hospital med-
ical imaging experiments with explicit privacy budgeting [10]. These studies confirm
that privacy can be integrated into medical FL, but they also reveal a recurring limi-
tation: privacy-preserving pipelines are rarely evaluated together with edge-hardware
constraints and complete bandwidth-latency trade-offs.
For FEDMED-X, DP-SGD is selected because it provides a mathematically in-
terpretable privacy budget (ϵ, δ) and can be implemented in practical deep learning
pipelines through frameworks such as Opacus.
This choice prioritizes measurable
privacy-utility analysis over heavier cryptographic alternatives that may be difficult
to deploy on low-resource clinical edge devices.
11


--- PAGE 31 ---

2.1.4
Communication Efficiency and Compression
Communication overhead is one of the most immediate barriers to real hospital FL.
Model updates can be large, rounds are repeated many times, and institutional net-
works may be restricted. Schoenpflug et al. document how network constraints and
operational complexity affect real multi-center FL deployments [4]. In response, multi-
ple studies investigate sparsification, quantization, and other compression strategies.
Habib et al. explore TopK-style sparsification for communication-efficient medical
FL habib2025cflsparsemed. Yesmin also reports substantial communication reduc-
tion in a privacy-preserving setting [9]. These works demonstrate that bandwidth can
be reduced significantly. However, compression is not free. Aggressive sparsification
can discard informative update components, and when combined with DP noise, the
interaction may further degrade utility. This interaction is exactly why FEDMED-X
treats compression as an experimental factor rather than an assumed improvement.
2.1.5
Edge Deployment and Clinical Translation
Even when FL algorithms perform well in simulation, clinical value depends on deploy-
ability. Bujotzek et al. show that real radiology FL initiatives face infrastructure and
process barriers beyond model accuracy [3]. Vincent and Surantha investigate federated
deep learning and edge inference for skin cancer classification on edge computing de-
vices, providing evidence that edge-oriented medical imaging workflows are feasible [11].
Nevertheless, edge papers often focus on inference speed or device feasibility and do not
always integrate formal DP accounting, non-IID robust optimization, and compression
analysis in one pipeline.
This separation of concerns is the key literature gap. Algorithmic FL papers, pri-
vacy papers, compression papers, and edge papers are abundant, but unified systems
12


--- PAGE 32 ---

evaluations remain scarce. Ghosh et al. explicitly note the shortage of deployment-
oriented evidence in medical FL research [7]. Zhou et al. similarly call for broader
evaluation criteria that include system cost dimensions [8]. FEDMED-X is designed in
direct response to this gap.
2.1.6
Lightweight Medical Models and Attention Mechanisms
Deployment on resource-constrained hardware requires compact models. Large back-
bones may achieve strong accuracy in cloud settings but are less suitable for low-power
clinical edge devices. For this reason, FEDMED-X uses a lightweight custom MedNet
architecture. To improve representational focus on diagnostically relevant regions, the
model incorporates the Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM), which com-
bines channel and spatial attention woo2018cbam. In addition, class imbalance is
common in medical datasets; Focal Loss is therefore used to emphasize difficult sam-
ples during training lin2017focal.
The dataset foundation of this project is MedMNIST v2, a standardized biomedical
image classification benchmark that supports reproducible experimentation yang2023medmnist.
Although MedMNIST images are smaller and more controlled than full-resolution clin-
ical scans, they provide a valid and widely used testbed for methodological comparison
and systems ablation under federated partitions.

2.2
Problem Analysis
The literature makes clear that the core problem is multi-dimensional. FEDMED-X
therefore analyzes the problem at four interdependent layers.
2.2.1
Statistical Problem: Non-IID Hospital Data
In multi-hospital learning, local datasets differ in size and class balance. Under Dirichlet
partitioning, some clients may contain dominant disease-positive samples while others
contain mostly normal cases. This imbalance causes local optima to diverge. Stan-
dard FedAvg aggregates these divergent updates without sufficiently constraining local
drift, which can reduce global stability. FedProx addresses this statistical problem by
penalizing deviation from the global model through a proximal term [2], [6].
2.2.2
Security Problem: Residual Gradient Leakage
Even if raw images never leave the hospital, shared updates may leak private informa-
tion. From a systems-security perspective, this means data locality is necessary but not
sufficient. Formal privacy mechanisms such as DP-SGD are required when regulatory
confidence and adversarial robustness matter abadi2016deep, [5]. The practical chal-
lenge is that stronger privacy noise usually reduces diagnostic utility. Hence privacy is
not a binary feature; it is a tunable trade-off that must be measured.
2.2.3
Systems Problem: Communication and Network Constraints
Hospital networks are not experimental testbeds. Firewalls, limited bandwidth, and
operational controls can delay or block heavy update exchange [3], [4]. Compression
techniques such as TopK sparsification and low-precision quantization reduce payload
size, but may also remove useful signal. When compression is combined with DP noise,
15


--- PAGE 35 ---

discarded coefficients may include information needed for recovery of model quality.
This creates a joint optimization problem rather than an independent add-on feature.
2.2.4
Deployment Problem: Edge Resource Limits
A clinically useful pipeline must eventually run in constrained environments. Rural and
semi-urban clinics cannot always depend on continuous cloud connectivity or high-end
GPUs. This creates demand for lightweight models, efficient inference, and deployment-
aware architecture choices [7], [11]. FEDMED-X therefore treats edge suitability as a
design requirement, not a postscript.
2.2.5
Integrated Problem Statement
Combining the four layers yields the integrated problem addressed by this project:
How can a federated medical image classification system preserve non-IID
robustness, enforce formal privacy, reduce communication cost, and remain
eligible for edge deployment, while quantifying the resulting accuracy trade-
offs under a controlled and reproducible experimental protocol?
FEDMED-X answers this question through systems integration and factorial evalu-
ation rather than through a claim of a single new optimizer. The expected contribution
is a practical evidence base that shows what performance remains when all major de-
ployment constraints are applied together.
2.2.6
Implications for Methodology
This problem analysis directly determines the methodological choices in Chapter 3:
1. use FedProx for non-IID stability;
2. use DP-SGD for formal privacy accounting;
16


--- PAGE 36 ---

3. use TopK and INT8 for communication reduction;
4. use MedNet-CBAM for lightweight representation learning;
5. evaluate all components through controlled ablation on MedMNIST federated par-
titions.
Summary
This chapter reviewed the background necessary to understand FEDMED-X. Prior re-
search provides strong foundations in medical federated learning, FedProx-based non-
IID optimization, differential privacy, communication compression, and edge-oriented
inference. However, these components are usually studied in separation. Clinical de-
ployment studies and surveys confirm that real hospital environments impose joint
constraints that isolated benchmarks do not capture. The resulting research gap is a
lack of unified, reproducible systems evaluation under non-IID, privacy, bandwidth, and
edge-related requirements. Chapter 3 converts this gap into a concrete methodology
for the FEDMED-X framework.

3.1
Proposed Framework
FEDMED-X is formulated as a multi-hospital federated learning framework with five
simulated clients. In every communication round, each client receives the current global
model, performs local training on its private partition, optionally applies differentially
private optimization, compresses the outgoing update, and sends the update to the
server. The server aggregates client updates by sample-size-weighted averaging and
broadcasts the new global model.
The framework is deliberately modular so that each design choice can be isolated
in ablation experiments:
1. Baseline aggregation path: FedAvg without privacy or compression;
2. Non-IID robust path: FedProx with proximal regularization;
3. Privacy path: FedProx + DP-SGD;
4. Compression path: FedProx + TopK/INT8;
5. Full systems path: FedProx + DP-SGD + TopK/INT8 (FEDMED-X).
This factorial structure is essential for scientific interpretability. It allows the project
to measure not only final accuracy, but also the marginal effect of privacy and com-
pression under the same data split, model family, and training protocol [8].
3.1.1
Design Principles
The proposed framework follows four engineering principles:
• Privacy by design: raw images never leave the client boundary;
• Robustness to heterogeneity: local optimization is constrained to reduce harmful
drift;

• Communication realism: update payloads are compressed before server trans-
mission;
• Edge eligibility: the backbone remains lightweight enough for resource-constrained
deployment targets such as NVIDIA Jetson Nano class devices.

3.3.5

When privacy mode is enabled, local optimization uses DP-SGD abadi2016deep. Per-

ϵ = 1.0,

3.3.6
TopK + INT8 Compression
To reduce communication cost, outgoing updates are compressed in two stages:
1. TopK sparsification (50%): only the highest-magnitude half of coefficients are
retained;
2. INT8 quantization simulation: retained values are quantized to 8-bit scale and
dequantized for aggregation compatibility.
The combined scheme is designed to approximate substantial payload reduction. In this
project, the full compressed setting is associated with a 90% bandwidth-saving target
relative to dense full-precision update exchange.

Data Preprocessing
PneumoniaMNIST images were loaded as grayscale samples of size 28 × 28. The pre-

4. Split usage: official train/validation/test splits were preserved;
5. Federated partitioning: only the training split was divided across clients.
For federated simulation, the training set was partitioned into five clients using a
Dirichlet distribution with α = 0.5. This produced non-IID label skew and unequal local
dataset sizes, which is representative of multi-hospital imbalance yang2023medmnist,
[6]. Validation and test splits remained global and were used for monitoring and final
evaluation, respectively.
In the executed experimental run, client sizes were highly imbalanced. One hospital
partition contained only a few samples, while another contained several thousand sam-
ples. This imbalance increased the difficulty of federated optimization and provided a
realistic stress test for FedAvg and FedProx.
4.3
Model Implementation
The classification backbone was a custom lightweight CNN named MedNet, enhanced
with CBAM attention woo2018cbam. The implementation used privacy-safe design
choices required by DP-SGD:
32


--- PAGE 52 ---

• BatchNorm layers were replaced by GroupNorm;
• ReLU activations were implemented with inplace=False;
• CBAM was inserted after early convolutional stages;
• the classifier used dropout regularization before the final linear layer.
These choices were not cosmetic. Standard BatchNorm is incompatible with per-
sample gradient accounting in DP-SGD because batch statistics couple samples to-
gether.
GroupNorm avoids this dependency and therefore preserves privacy-engine
compatibility. Non-inplace activations were required to prevent autograd conflicts un-
der Opacus backward hooks.
Local training minimized Focal Loss to reduce the effect of easy majority samples
and emphasize difficult cases lin2017focal. For FedProx runs, an additional proximal
term was added:
Ltotal = Lfocal + µ
2∥w −wglobal∥2

where µ = 0.01.
When DP-SGD was enabled, Opacus wrapped the local model and optimizer, clipped
gradients, and injected calibrated Gaussian noise for target privacy budget ϵ = 1.0 and
δ = 1 × 10−5 abadi2016deep. When compression was enabled, client updates were
processed by TopK 50% sparsification followed by INT8 quantization simulation before
aggregation.
4.4
Evaluation Metrics
Model quality was evaluated on the held-out PneumoniaMNIST test set after the final
communication round of each ablation. The following metrics were computed:
33


--- PAGE 53 ---

• Accuracy: overall proportion of correctly classified test samples;
• Macro-F1 Score: harmonic mean of precision and recall averaged equally across
classes;
• Precision: reliability of positive predictions;
• Recall: sensitivity to true positive cases;
• Privacy mode: whether DP-SGD was enabled and the target ϵ;
• Bandwidth mode: whether TopK/INT8 compression was enabled;
• Runtime: end-to-end training time for the ablation configuration.
Macro-F1 is particularly important in medical settings because accuracy alone can
hide class-wise weakness under imbalance. For this reason, both Accuracy and Macro-
F1 are reported as primary utility metrics.

4.6
Key Findings and Discussions
The experimental results yield five central findings.
1. FedProx is a better non-IID baseline than FedAvg in this setting. The im-
provement from A1 to A2 confirms that proximal regularization helps under Dirichlet
hospital imbalance.
2. Privacy has a measurable but bounded isolated cost. At ϵ = 1.0, DP-SGD
reduces utility modestly while providing formal privacy accounting.
3. Compression can improve both efficiency and utility. A4 achieved the best
overall non-private scores while reducing communication burden by 90%.
4. Joint constraints are harder than single constraints. The full stack remains
functional at 75.16% accuracy, but the combined privacy-compression interaction
introduces a substantially larger utility tax than DP alone.
5. Runtime overhead is dominated by privacy, not compression. DP-enabled
runs are several times slower than non-private runs, which is an important systems
cost for practical scheduling.
From a research perspective, these findings support the thesis statement of FEDMED-
X: the value of the work lies in quantifying trade-offs under joint constraints, not in
claiming unconstrained state-of-the-art accuracy.
A system that reports only non-
private uncompressed accuracy would overstate deployment readiness. Conversely, a
system that reports only full-stack accuracy without ablation would hide the source of
performance loss. The factorial design avoids both errors.

Sustainability Standards
Sustainability in this project is interpreted in three layers: environmental sustainability,
operational sustainability, and academic reproducibility.
5.1.1
Environmental Sustainability
Centralized medical AI pipelines often depend on repeated cloud-side data transfer and
large-scale computation. FEDMED-X reduces unnecessary communication by applying
TopK sparsification and INT8 quantization, with a target bandwidth reduction of 90%
in the compressed configurations. Lower communication volume reduces network energy
cost across federated rounds. In addition, the MedNet-CBAM backbone is deliberately
lightweight, which supports inference on low-power edge hardware such as NVIDIA
Jetson Nano class devices rather than continuous dependence on high-power cloud
GPUs [8], [11].

From a sustainability standpoint, this design encourages:
• fewer bytes transferred per communication round;
• lower reliance on centralized high-energy infrastructure for inference;
• longer-term feasibility in clinics with limited power and network budgets.

Operational Sustainability
A system is operationally sustainable only if institutions can maintain it under ordi-
nary constraints. FEDMED-X supports this goal through modular design. Privacy,
compression, and optimizer choices can be enabled or disabled independently. This
allows a hospital network to start with a simpler configuration and gradually adopt
stronger privacy or compression settings as local capacity matures. The factorial ab-
lation protocol also improves maintainability because future teams can re-run A1–A5
comparisons after changes in data, hardware, or hyperparameters.

In research-engineering projects, reproducibility is part of sustainability. Without clear
logs, seeds, and modular code, a promising prototype cannot be extended. FEDMED-

with the broader call in medical FL literature for stronger evaluation and reporting

Conclusion
This capstone project designed, implemented, and evaluated FEDMED-X, a communication-
efficient and privacy-preserving federated learning pipeline for medical image classifica-
tion under edge-oriented constraints. The central premise of the work was that prac-
tical medical FL systems cannot be judged by accuracy alone. In real multi-hospital
environments, non-IID data drift, residual gradient leakage, bandwidth limitations, and
resource-constrained deployment requirements appear simultaneously. Therefore, a use-
ful engineering contribution must quantify performance under joint constraints rather
than under isolated best-case conditions [1], [3], [7].
FEDMED-X addressed this need through a modular systems architecture. A lightweight
MedNet-CBAM backbone was trained across five simulated hospital clients created by
Dirichlet non-IID partitioning of PneumoniaMNIST. FedProx was used to stabilize het-
erogeneous local updates, DP-SGD provided formal privacy accounting at ϵ = 1.0, and
TopK+INT8 compression reduced communication burden by 90% in compressed con-
figurations. The project then evaluated these components through a controlled five-run
factorial ablation.
The experimental findings validate the core thesis. FedProx outperformed FedAvg
under non-IID conditions, improving accuracy from 84.46% to 85.58% and Macro-F1
from 81.78% to 83.26%. Adding DP-SGD alone introduced a bounded privacy tax, re-
ducing accuracy to 84.13%. Compression alone was highly effective, achieving 87.98%
accuracy with 90% bandwidth reduction. When privacy and compression were applied
together in the full FEDMED-X stack, the system retained usable diagnostic signal at
54


--- PAGE 74 ---

75.16% accuracy while enforcing both ϵ = 1.0 privacy and 90% communication reduc-
tion. This joint result is lower than the non-private baselines, but it is also more honest:
it represents the cost of deploying federated medical learning under simultaneous real-
world constraints.
From an engineering perspective, the project also demonstrated that implementation
details are decisive. Privacy-safe design required GroupNorm instead of BatchNorm and
non-inplace activations for stable DP-SGD training. Extreme client imbalance under
Dirichlet partitioning confirmed why naive aggregation is insufficient. Runtime anal-
ysis showed that privacy, not compression, dominates computational overhead. These
observations reinforce that FEDMED-X is a complex systems problem involving in-
terdependent choices across model design, optimization, privacy, communication, and
deployment planning abadi2016deep, [2], [4].
In societal terms, the work contributes a privacy-aware pathway for collaborative
medical AI in resource-constrained settings. By keeping raw data local, reducing com-
munication volume, and targeting edge-eligible models, FEDMED-X aligns with the
needs of hospitals and clinics that cannot support unrestricted data sharing or high-
cost infrastructure.
At the same time, the report intentionally avoids overclaiming
clinical readiness. The system is a research prototype and decision-support foundation,
not a certified autonomous diagnostic device.
Overall, FEDMED-X achieves its intended contribution: a reproducible, deployment-
aware federated medical learning framework that measures the privacy–utility–communication
trade-off with engineering rigor.
6.1
Limitations
Despite its contributions, FEDMED-X has important limitations that must be stated
clearly.

--- PAGE 75 ---

1. Benchmark-scale dataset: Experiments were conducted on PneumoniaMNIST
from MedMNIST v2.
This supports reproducibility and controlled ablation, but
28 × 28 benchmark images do not capture the full complexity of high-resolution
clinical radiographs yang2023medmnist.
2. Simulated federation: The five hospital clients were simulated through Dirich-
let partitioning rather than deployed as live geographically distributed institutions.
Real hospital networks introduce additional operational issues such as downtime,
authentication, routing policy, and governance overhead [3], [4].
3. Limited task coverage: The main reported ablation focuses on binary pneumonia
classification. Broader validation across multi-class tasks such as DermaMNIST and
BloodMNIST would strengthen generality.
4. Joint-constraint utility drop: Under simultaneous DP-SGD and TopK/INT8
compression, accuracy and especially recall decline relative to non-private baselines.
This means the full stack is more conservative and can miss more positive cases if
used without calibration.
5. Training-deployment asymmetry: Model training was performed on Colab GPUs.
Edge evaluation is currently profile-oriented toward Jetson Nano class inference and
does not yet include a complete on-device federated training study.
6. Single-seed experimental scope: The reported master table reflects the com-
pleted controlled run family used in this capstone. Stronger statistical reporting
would include multi-seed means and standard deviations.
7. No prospective clinical validation: The system has not been validated in clinician-
in-the-loop trials, so medical utility remains experimental rather than clinical evi-
dence of bedside effectiveness.

These limitations do not invalidate the results. They define the correct interpreta-

A full physical pipeline on NVIDIA Jetson Nano or similar devices should measure
latency, memory, power, and thermal behavior under realistic inference loads. Beyond
single-device profiling, a real multi-site pilot with institutional partners would expose
operational constraints not visible in simulation [3], [11].
6.2.5
Clinical Workflow Integration
Final translation requires integration with hospital PACS/RIS workflows, role-based
access control, audit logs, clinician feedback interfaces, and regulatory documentation.
At that stage, FEDMED-X would move from research prototype to clinical pilot infras-
tructure, subject to ethics approval and formal validation.
6.2.6
Long-Term Research Vision
In the long term, FEDMED-X can evolve into a general trustworthy edge-FL platform
for low-resource healthcare systems, combining:
• non-IID robust optimization;
• formal privacy guarantees;
• bandwidth-aware communication;
• edge-first model design;
• transparent multi-objective reporting.
This direction is aligned with global needs for privacy-preserving medical AI and with
graduate research themes in trustworthy machine learning and distributed systems.

Summary
This chapter concluded the FEDMED-X capstone report.
The project successfully
implemented a unified federated medical learning pipeline and evaluated it under non-
IID, privacy, and communication constraints. The results show that FedProx improves

stronger statistics, privacy-compression co-design, physical edge deployment, and clini-

foundation for trustworthy, communication-efficient, and edge-aware federated medical
image classification.
