Abstract
Federated Learning (FL) enables collaborative medical image diagnosis without sharing
raw patient data, supporting privacy regulations such as HIPAA and GDPR. In practice,
medical FL faces non-IID data drift, communication bottlenecks, and gradient-leakage
risks. Existing studies often address these issues separately and rarely evaluate full
pipelines under edge constraints.
This report presents FEDMED-X, a communication-efficient and privacy-preserving
federated learning system for medical image classification. The framework integrates
FedProx (µ = 0.01), DP-SGD (ϵ = 1.0, δ = 1e−5), TopK 50% + INT8 compression,
and a lightweight MedNet-CBAM model designed for edge deployment.
On MedMNIST PneumoniaMNIST with 5 Dirichlet non-IID clients (α = 0.5), FedProx reached 85.58% accuracy. With privacy and compression together, FEDMED-X
achieved 75.16% accuracy while reducing communication by 90% under ϵ = 1.0. Edgeoriented evaluation targets NVIDIA Jetson Nano deployment with 0.20 ms/image inference latency.
Keywords: Federated Learning; Differential Privacy; Edge Computing; Medical Image
Classification; FedProx; TopK; Jetson Nano.

Chapter 1

1

Introduction

Artificial Intelligence (AI) has transformed medical image analysis by enabling automated detection of diseases from radiological scans such as chest X-rays. Deep learning
models can support clinicians in identifying pneumonia and related abnormalities with
high speed and consistency. However, building robust medical AI systems requires large
and diverse datasets collected across multiple hospitals. In practice, hospitals cannot
freely pool patient images into a single central repository because of legal, ethical, and
security constraints.
Privacy regulations such as the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability
Act (HIPAA) and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) restrict the transfer of identifiable medical data. As a result, conventional centralized machine learning pipelines face a fundamental conflict: high model performance requires multiinstitutional data, while privacy law requires data localization. Federated Learning
(FL) was introduced to resolve this conflict by allowing institutions to train a shared
model collaboratively while keeping raw patient data inside each hospital [1], [2].
Despite strong theoretical promise, real-world medical FL deployments remain difficult. Recent multi-center studies report that hospital firewalls, bandwidth limits,
non-Independent and Identically Distributed (non-IID) data drift, and residual gradient leakage frequently block practical translation [3], [4], [5]. In resource-constrained
settings such as rural Bangladesh, these barriers are more severe because clinics often
lack high-end computing infrastructure and stable high-bandwidth networks. Therefore,
medical FL systems must be simultaneously accurate, private, communication-efficient,
and edge-deployable.
1

This capstone project presents FEDMED-X, a unified federated learning pipeline
for medical image classification that jointly integrates FedProx optimization, Differential Privacy via DP-SGD, TopK+INT8 compression, and a lightweight MedNet-CBAM
neural backbone for edge-oriented deployment. Rather than proposing an isolated algorithm, FEDMED-X contributes a systems-level evaluation of the privacy–utility–
communication trade-off under realistic non-IID hospital partitions.

1.1

Problem Statement

The central problem addressed in this research is the absence of a practical, end-to-end
federated medical imaging pipeline that can operate under joint real-world constraints.
Existing solutions typically optimize only one dimension of the problem. Some methods
improve non-IID robustness, some add privacy noise, some compress communication
payloads, and some evaluate edge inference. Very few systems evaluate all of these
requirements together under a common experimental protocol.
In clinical environments, this gap creates four concrete failures:
1. Privacy failure: Centralized training requires raw image aggregation and increases
exposure to data breaches.
2. Heterogeneity failure: Hospital datasets differ by patient population, scanner
type, and disease prevalence, causing standard FedAvg models to drift.
3. Communication failure: Full-precision gradient exchange is expensive and often
blocked by institutional firewalls.
4. Deployment failure: Heavy models and privacy mechanisms are difficult to run
on low-cost edge devices suitable for rural clinics.

2

Therefore, the research problem is not only to classify medical images, but to design
a federated system that remains usable when privacy, non-IID drift, bandwidth limits,
and edge hardware constraints are enforced at the same time.

1.2

Problem Background

Medical imaging AI has achieved strong benchmark performance under centralized
training. However, healthcare data are naturally fragmented. Each hospital stores
its own patient records, and cross-hospital transfer is restricted by regulation and institutional policy [1]. FL addresses this by exchanging model updates instead of raw
images [2].
Unfortunately, recent clinical evidence shows that FL prototypes often fail during
translation. Bujotzek et al. demonstrated that infrastructure, governance, and datamanagement bottlenecks remain major obstacles in multi-hospital radiology FL [3].
Schoenpflug et al. reported that network restrictions and long communication delays
significantly affect multi-country pathology FL deployments [4]. Koutsoubis et al. further emphasized that keeping data local is not equivalent to full privacy, because shared
gradients can still leak sensitive information [5].
From an algorithmic perspective, non-IID data remains a dominant challenge. Riyadi
et al. showed that FedProx is more robust than FedAvg under Dirichlet label skew on
MedMNIST tasks [6]. At the same time, surveys highlight weak clinical and edge
deployment benchmarks in current medical FL literature [7], [8]. These findings collectively motivate a system that combines non-IID-aware optimization, formal privacy,
communication compression, and edge-oriented model design.

1.3

Research Objectives

The objectives of this study are as follows:
3

(i) To design a federated learning framework that enables multiple simulated hospitals to collaboratively train a shared medical image classifier without exchanging
raw patient data.
(ii) To mitigate non-IID client drift using FedProx regularization with proximal coefficient µ = 0.01.
(iii) To enforce formal privacy protection through DP-SGD with target privacy budget
ϵ = 1.0 and δ = 1 × 10−5 .
(iv) To reduce communication overhead by applying TopK 50% gradient sparsification
and INT8 quantization, targeting approximately 90% bandwidth reduction.
(v) To evaluate the complete pipeline through a factorial ablation study and assess
edge-oriented deployability using a lightweight MedNet-CBAM model suitable for
NVIDIA Jetson Nano class hardware.

1.4

Motivations

This research is motivated by three complementary needs.
First, there is a clinical motivation. Bangladesh and many low-resource regions
face shortages of specialist radiologists. Affordable AI tools deployed at the edge can
support faster triage of chest X-rays in rural clinics, provided patient privacy is preserved.
Second, there is a technical motivation. Most published medical FL studies
evaluate components in isolation. A practical system must quantify what happens when
privacy noise, compression, and non-IID optimization are applied together. Without
this joint evaluation, reported accuracies can be misleading for real deployment.
Third, there is an academic and societal motivation. Trustworthy AI, privacypreserving learning, and edge intelligence are priority themes in modern computing
4

research and graduate study pathways. FEDMED-X is designed as a reproducible
systems benchmark that connects algorithm design with deployment reality.

1.5

Significance of the Research

The significance of FEDMED-X can be summarized in four dimensions:
• Scientific significance: It provides a controlled multi-objective evaluation of accuracy, privacy budget, communication cost, and edge suitability under non-IID
medical FL settings.
• Engineering significance: It converts known components (FedProx, DP-SGD,
TopK, CBAM) into a coherent pipeline with explicit trade-off measurement.
• Healthcare significance: It supports privacy-preserving collaborative learning
that can benefit hospitals unwilling or unable to share raw data.
• Educational significance: It demonstrates complex engineering problem-solving
across machine learning, distributed systems, privacy mathematics, and embedded
deployment constraints.

1.6

Research Contributions

The major contributions of this capstone research are:
1. Unified systems pipeline: Development of FEDMED-X, integrating FedProx,
DP-SGD, TopK+INT8 compression, and MedNet-CBAM in one reproducible federated medical classification framework.
2. Factorial ablation protocol: Design and execution of a five-run ablation matrix
(FedAvg, FedProx, FedProx+DP, FedProx+TopK/INT8, and full FEDMED-X) to
isolate privacy and compression effects.
5

3. Empirical trade-off evidence: Demonstration that FedProx improves non-IID
robustness over FedAvg (85.58% vs 84.46% accuracy), while full privacy and compression constraints yield 75.16% accuracy with 90% bandwidth reduction under
ϵ = 1.0.
4. Edge-oriented design: Construction of a lightweight, Opacus-compatible GroupNormbased MedNet-CBAM backbone targeting resource-constrained deployment scenarios such as NVIDIA Jetson Nano.
5. Deployment-aware framing: Positioning the work as a systems evaluation contribution rather than an unsupported absolute “first invention” claim, aligned with
current literature gaps in edge and clinical translation [7], [8].

1.7

Complex Engineering Analysis

FEDMED-X qualifies as a complex engineering project because it cannot be solved by
a single textbook method. It requires concurrent reasoning over conflicting objectives,
multi-disciplinary knowledge, and interdependent subsystems.
1.7.1

Complex Engineering Problems

The project maps to the following complex engineering problem attributes:
• Depth of knowledge: The solution requires machine learning, distributed optimization, differential privacy, medical data handling, and edge computing.
• Conflicting requirements: Higher privacy noise and stronger compression generally reduce accuracy; higher accuracy often increases communication and compute
cost.

6

• Depth of analysis: Multiple design alternatives exist (FedAvg vs FedProx, DP vs
no DP, compressed vs uncompressed updates), requiring controlled ablation rather
than one-shot implementation.
• Unfamiliarity: Formal DP accounting, non-IID federated optimization, and edge
deployment constraints are beyond routine undergraduate coursework.
• Stakeholder diversity: Patients, clinicians, hospital IT units, regulators, and rural
health workers impose different success criteria.
• Interdependence: Model architecture, privacy engine, compression module, and
communication protocol affect one another and must be calibrated jointly.
1.7.2

Complex Engineering Activities

The complex engineering activities performed in this project include:
• Resource integration: Combining Colab GPU training, MedMNIST datasets,
Flower/PyTorch tooling, and edge-oriented model constraints.
• Trade-off resolution: Measuring privacy tax, compression effects, and joint utility
loss through factorial experiments.
• Innovation through synthesis: Building an end-to-end evaluation framework
that connects algorithm choice to deployment metrics.
• Consequence analysis: Assessing societal impact for privacy-preserving healthcare AI in low-resource environments.
• Extended practice: Applying research literature synthesis, reproducible experimental design, and professional technical documentation.

Chapter 2

Background Study

This chapter establishes the theoretical and empirical foundation of FEDMED-X. First,
it reviews the major research streams relevant to federated medical image classification: non-IID robust optimization, privacy-preserving learning, communication-efficient
model updates, and edge-oriented deployment. Second, it analyzes the practical problem space and identifies the systems gap that motivates the proposed framework. The
goal of this chapter is not only to summarize prior work, but to show why isolated solutions are insufficient for real hospital constraints and why a joint evaluation pipeline
is required.

2.1

Literature Review

Federated Learning (FL) has become a central paradigm for collaborative healthcare AI
because it enables multi-institutional training without direct exchange of raw patient
images [1]. In medical imaging, this property is especially important due to strict legal
and ethical constraints on data movement. Over the last several years, the literature
has expanded from basic aggregation methods toward specialized solutions for statistical heterogeneity, formal privacy, communication bottlenecks, and deployment realism.
The following subsections synthesize these directions and position FEDMED-X within
the resulting research landscape.
2.1.1

Federated Learning for Medical Imaging

Guan and Liu provide a comprehensive survey of federated medical image analysis and
organize the field around system architecture, privacy mechanisms, and communication
9

challenges [1]. Their review makes clear that medical FL is not a single-algorithm
problem; it is a multi-constraint systems problem. Ghosh et al. further emphasize that
despite rapid publication growth, clinical translation remains limited and deploymentoriented benchmarks are still weak [7]. Zhou et al. reinforce this point by arguing that
fair evaluation of medical FL should include not only accuracy, but also communication
and computational efficiency [8].
These surveys collectively indicate a shift in research expectations. A method that
reports only centralized-style accuracy under simplified conditions is no longer sufficient.
Modern medical FL research is increasingly judged by whether it remains robust under
non-IID partitions, whether it supports privacy constraints, and whether it can operate
under practical resource limits.
2.1.2

Non-IID Heterogeneity and FedProx

A major obstacle in multi-hospital learning is non-IID data. Different institutions
observe different class distributions, acquisition protocols, and patient demographics.
Under such heterogeneity, standard Federated Averaging (FedAvg) often suffers from
client drift and unstable convergence. Li et al. introduced FedProx to address heterogeneous federated optimization by adding a proximal term that penalizes local deviation
from the global model [2]. This modification is particularly relevant to clinical networks
where local datasets may be small, unbalanced, or distributionally skewed.
Riyadi et al. provide direct empirical support for FedProx in medical imaging benchmarks. In a comparative study on MedMNIST tasks under Dirichlet label skew, FedProx demonstrated stronger robustness and stability than FedAvg and other selected
baselines [6]. This evidence motivates the use of FedProx as the core optimizer in
FEDMED-X. However, Riyadi et al. do not jointly evaluate formal differential privacy,

10

aggressive update compression, and edge deployment constraints. Therefore, their results establish FedProx as a strong non-IID baseline, but not as a complete deployment
solution.
2.1.3

Privacy Risks and Differential Privacy

A common misconception in federated healthcare systems is that data locality automatically guarantees privacy. In reality, shared gradients and model updates can still leak
sensitive information through reconstruction and membership-inference style attacks.
Koutsoubis et al. highlight this residual risk and argue for stronger privacy-preserving
FL designs in medical imaging [5]. Abadi et al. established Differentially Private
Stochastic Gradient Descent (DP-SGD) as a foundational method for training deep
networks with formal privacy guarantees by clipping gradients and injecting calibrated
noise abadi2016deep.
In the medical FL literature, privacy-preserving methods vary widely in strength and
practicality. Some frameworks rely on cryptographic techniques such as homomorphic
encryption, while others employ formal DP budgets. Yesmin proposed MedHE, combining communication efficiency with privacy-preserving mechanisms including DP and
homomorphic encryption [9]. Pachlasiya and Patel reported private multi-hospital medical imaging experiments with explicit privacy budgeting [10]. These studies confirm
that privacy can be integrated into medical FL, but they also reveal a recurring limitation: privacy-preserving pipelines are rarely evaluated together with edge-hardware
constraints and complete bandwidth-latency trade-offs.
For FEDMED-X, DP-SGD is selected because it provides a mathematically interpretable privacy budget (ϵ, δ) and can be implemented in practical deep learning
pipelines through frameworks such as Opacus.

This choice prioritizes measurable

privacy-utility analysis over heavier cryptographic alternatives that may be difficult
to deploy on low-resource clinical edge devices.
11

2.1.4

Communication Efficiency and Compression

Communication overhead is one of the most immediate barriers to real hospital FL.
Model updates can be large, rounds are repeated many times, and institutional networks may be restricted. Schoenpflug et al. document how network constraints and
operational complexity affect real multi-center FL deployments [4]. In response, multiple studies investigate sparsification, quantization, and other compression strategies.
Habib et al. explore TopK-style sparsification for communication-efficient medical
FL habib2025cflsparsemed. Yesmin also reports substantial communication reduction in a privacy-preserving setting [9]. These works demonstrate that bandwidth can
be reduced significantly. However, compression is not free. Aggressive sparsification
can discard informative update components, and when combined with DP noise, the
interaction may further degrade utility. This interaction is exactly why FEDMED-X
treats compression as an experimental factor rather than an assumed improvement.
2.1.5

Edge Deployment and Clinical Translation

Even when FL algorithms perform well in simulation, clinical value depends on deployability. Bujotzek et al. show that real radiology FL initiatives face infrastructure and
process barriers beyond model accuracy [3]. Vincent and Surantha investigate federated
deep learning and edge inference for skin cancer classification on edge computing devices, providing evidence that edge-oriented medical imaging workflows are feasible [11].
Nevertheless, edge papers often focus on inference speed or device feasibility and do not
always integrate formal DP accounting, non-IID robust optimization, and compression
analysis in one pipeline.
This separation of concerns is the key literature gap. Algorithmic FL papers, privacy papers, compression papers, and edge papers are abundant, but unified systems

12

evaluations remain scarce. Ghosh et al. explicitly note the shortage of deploymentoriented evidence in medical FL research [7]. Zhou et al. similarly call for broader
evaluation criteria that include system cost dimensions [8]. FEDMED-X is designed in
direct response to this gap.
2.1.6

Lightweight Medical Models and Attention Mechanisms

Deployment on resource-constrained hardware requires compact models. Large backbones may achieve strong accuracy in cloud settings but are less suitable for low-power
clinical edge devices. For this reason, FEDMED-X uses a lightweight custom MedNet
architecture. To improve representational focus on diagnostically relevant regions, the
model incorporates the Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM), which combines channel and spatial attention woo2018cbam. In addition, class imbalance is
common in medical datasets; Focal Loss is therefore used to emphasize difficult samples during training lin2017focal.
The dataset foundation of this project is MedMNIST v2, a standardized biomedical
image classification benchmark that supports reproducible experimentation yang2023medmnist.
Although MedMNIST images are smaller and more controlled than full-resolution clinical scans, they provide a valid and widely used testbed for methodological comparison
and systems ablation under federated partitions.

Problem Analysis

The literature makes clear that the core problem is multi-dimensional. FEDMED-X
therefore analyzes the problem at four interdependent layers.
2.2.1

Statistical Problem: Non-IID Hospital Data

In multi-hospital learning, local datasets differ in size and class balance. Under Dirichlet
partitioning, some clients may contain dominant disease-positive samples while others
contain mostly normal cases. This imbalance causes local optima to diverge. Standard FedAvg aggregates these divergent updates without sufficiently constraining local
drift, which can reduce global stability. FedProx addresses this statistical problem by
penalizing deviation from the global model through a proximal term [2], [6].
2.2.2

Security Problem: Residual Gradient Leakage

Even if raw images never leave the hospital, shared updates may leak private information. From a systems-security perspective, this means data locality is necessary but not
sufficient. Formal privacy mechanisms such as DP-SGD are required when regulatory
confidence and adversarial robustness matter abadi2016deep, [5]. The practical challenge is that stronger privacy noise usually reduces diagnostic utility. Hence privacy is
not a binary feature; it is a tunable trade-off that must be measured.
2.2.3

Systems Problem: Communication and Network Constraints

Hospital networks are not experimental testbeds. Firewalls, limited bandwidth, and
operational controls can delay or block heavy update exchange [3], [4]. Compression
techniques such as TopK sparsification and low-precision quantization reduce payload
size, but may also remove useful signal. When compression is combined with DP noise,

15

discarded coefficients may include information needed for recovery of model quality.
This creates a joint optimization problem rather than an independent add-on feature.
2.2.4

Deployment Problem: Edge Resource Limits

A clinically useful pipeline must eventually run in constrained environments. Rural and
semi-urban clinics cannot always depend on continuous cloud connectivity or high-end
GPUs. This creates demand for lightweight models, efficient inference, and deploymentaware architecture choices [7], [11]. FEDMED-X therefore treats edge suitability as a
design requirement, not a postscript.
2.2.5

Integrated Problem Statement

Combining the four layers yields the integrated problem addressed by this project:
How can a federated medical image classification system preserve non-IID
robustness, enforce formal privacy, reduce communication cost, and remain
eligible for edge deployment, while quantifying the resulting accuracy tradeoffs under a controlled and reproducible experimental protocol?
FEDMED-X answers this question through systems integration and factorial evaluation rather than through a claim of a single new optimizer. The expected contribution
is a practical evidence base that shows what performance remains when all major deployment constraints are applied together.
2.2.6

Implications for Methodology

This problem analysis directly determines the methodological choices in Chapter 3:
1. use FedProx for non-IID stability;
2. use DP-SGD for formal privacy accounting;
16

3. use TopK and INT8 for communication reduction;
4. use MedNet-CBAM for lightweight representation learning;
5. evaluate all components through controlled ablation on MedMNIST federated partitions.

Summary
This chapter reviewed the background necessary to understand FEDMED-X. Prior research provides strong foundations in medical federated learning, FedProx-based nonIID optimization, differential privacy, communication compression, and edge-oriented
inference. However, these components are usually studied in separation. Clinical deployment studies and surveys confirm that real hospital environments impose joint
constraints that isolated benchmarks do not capture. The resulting research gap is a
lack of unified, reproducible systems evaluation under non-IID, privacy, bandwidth, and
edge-related requirements. Chapter 3 converts this gap into a concrete methodology
for the FEDMED-X framework.

3.1

Proposed Framework

FEDMED-X is formulated as a multi-hospital federated learning framework with five
simulated clients. In every communication round, each client receives the current global
model, performs local training on its private partition, optionally applies differentially
private optimization, compresses the outgoing update, and sends the update to the
server. The server aggregates client updates by sample-size-weighted averaging and
broadcasts the new global model.
The framework is deliberately modular so that each design choice can be isolated
in ablation experiments:
1. Baseline aggregation path: FedAvg without privacy or compression;
2. Non-IID robust path: FedProx with proximal regularization;
3. Privacy path: FedProx + DP-SGD;
4. Compression path: FedProx + TopK/INT8;
5. Full systems path: FedProx + DP-SGD + TopK/INT8 (FEDMED-X).
This factorial structure is essential for scientific interpretability. It allows the project
to measure not only final accuracy, but also the marginal effect of privacy and compression under the same data split, model family, and training protocol [8].
3.1.1

Design Principles

The proposed framework follows four engineering principles:
• Privacy by design: raw images never leave the client boundary;
• Robustness to heterogeneity: local optimization is constrained to reduce harmful
drift;
19

• Communication realism: update payloads are compressed before server transmission;
• Edge eligibility: the backbone remains lightweight enough for resource-constrained
deployment targets such as NVIDIA Jetson Nano class devices.

3.3.1

MedNet-CBAM Backbone

The classification backbone is a custom lightweight CNN named MedNet, augmented
with CBAM attention woo2018cbam. The network is intentionally compact to remain
eligible for edge deployment. Important implementation choices include:
• GroupNorm instead of BatchNorm for DP compatibility;
• non-inplace ReLU activations for stable autograd under Opacus;
• CBAM blocks after early convolutional stages to emphasize informative regions.

DP-SGD Privacy Mechanism

When privacy mode is enabled, local optimization uses DP-SGD abadi2016deep. Persample gradients are clipped to a maximum norm C and Gaussian noise is added before

ϵ = 1.0,

Privacy accounting is handled through Opacus with Rényi Differential Privacy. This
provides a formal interpretation of privacy loss rather than an informal claim of confidentiality.
3.3.6

TopK + INT8 Compression

To reduce communication cost, outgoing updates are compressed in two stages:
1. TopK sparsification (50%): only the highest-magnitude half of coefficients are
retained;
2. INT8 quantization simulation: retained values are quantized to 8-bit scale and
dequantized for aggregation compatibility.
The combined scheme is designed to approximate substantial payload reduction. In this
project, the full compressed setting is associated with a 90% bandwidth-saving target
relative to dense full-precision update exchange.

3.4

Explanation of Different Modules

FEDMED-X is implemented as a set of interdependent modules. Each module encapsulates one responsibility in the pipeline.
3.4.1

Data Module

The data module performs dataset download, tensor transformation, normalization,
and Dirichlet partitioning. Normalization uses mean and standard deviation of 0.5 for
grayscale inputs. The module outputs:
• client-wise training dataloaders;
• global validation loader;
• global test loader.

26

3.4.2

Model Module

The model module defines MedNet-CBAM and exposes a common interface for training
and inference. GroupNorm and non-inplace activations are mandatory design decisions
for DP compatibility and stable backward computation under privacy engines.
3.4.3

Federated Training Module

This module coordinates communication rounds. For each round it:
1. broadcasts global weights;
2. trains selected clients locally;
3. collects updates;
4. aggregates weights;
5. evaluates global performance.
3.4.4

Privacy Module

The privacy module wraps local training with Opacus DP-SGD when enabled. It
controls clipping, noise assignment, and target ϵ. In full-stack mode, privacy is applied
before update compression.
3.4.5

Compression Module

The compression module applies TopK masking and INT8 quantization simulation on
model updates. It can be disabled for baseline runs to measure compression-only effects.

3.4.6

Evaluation and Logging Module

The evaluation module computes metrics and stores experiment-level summaries, including accuracy, F1-score, precision, recall, privacy mode, compression mode, and
runtime. These logs are later used in Chapter 4 for result analysis and discussion.
3.4.7

Edge-Oriented Deployment Module

Although training is performed in a GPU cloud environment, the methodology includes
an edge-oriented deployment perspective. The model footprint and inference-oriented
design choices are aligned with NVIDIA Jetson Nano class constraints. The intended
inference target characteristics used in this project are ultra-low per-image latency and
high throughput on constrained hardware.
